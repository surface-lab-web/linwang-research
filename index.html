Research

Natural surfaces carry micro- and nanoarchitectures that achieve interfacial functions conventional design principles do not fully explain. A leafhopper coats itself in hollow, perforated particles that suppress reflection; water-repellent insects rely on densely packed nanotextures that classical wetting theory considers ineffective. My research investigates the physics of architected interfaces: how natural micro- and nanoarchitectures govern light–matter interactions, wetting, adhesion, and mechanical robustness, and how the underlying mechanisms can be translated into functional materials. I rebuild these architectures in controllable synthetic form using microscale 3D printing, cleanroom lithography, and surface functionalization, vary one geometric feature at a time, and separate the effects of geometry from those of material composition and surface chemistry.

Past Research

Insect-Inspired Particles That Control Infrared Light

Leafhoppers coat their bodies with tiny hollow, soccer-ball-like particles called brochosomes that suppress light reflection. By 3D printing synthetic versions with controlled size and pores, this work revealed how their geometry shapes reflection across different wavelengths and produced coatings that cut infrared reflection by up to 94%. The same structures can also tune how much heat a surface radiates and carry hidden patterns that only an infrared camera can read.

PNAS 2024 (cover) · Science Advances 2024

Line Tension and Droplet Impact Dynamics

Springtails stay dry with densely packed nanoscale textures, a design that classical wetting theory considers ineffective. This work showed that a tiny force along the edge of a droplet, known as line tension, makes droplets bounce off these textures faster, while the surface withstands impact pressures about an order of magnitude higher than conventional water-repellent surfaces can.

Science Advances 2020

Slippery Coatings for Water Sustainability

Pitcher plants catch insects with a slippery rim covered by a thin liquid layer. A coating inspired by this design holds a stable lubricant layer on the surface, so liquids, sticky solids, and bacteria slide off. On toilets, it reduces the water needed for flushing by about 90%, and it has been commercialized by spotLESS Materials.

Nature Sustainability 2019

Future Directions

My past work shows that micro- and nanoscale geometry can produce interfacial functions that conventional design rules do not predict. Looking ahead, my future research will move beyond organism-by-organism discoveries toward general design principles for architected interfaces: principles that hold across materials and operating conditions, that unite interfacial function with mechanical robustness, and that can be discovered systematically through fabrication, simulation, and AI.

Lin Wang

His research explores how natural micro- and nanoarchitectures govern interfacial phenomena, including light–matter interactions, wetting, adhesion, and mechanical robustness, and how the underlying mechanisms can be translated into functional materials. He received his Ph.D. in Materials Science and Engineering from Penn State, where he also completed postdoctoral training in the Department of Mechanical Engineering with Prof. Tak-Sing Wong. He then worked on functional surface coatings at Alcon and Guardian Glass.
